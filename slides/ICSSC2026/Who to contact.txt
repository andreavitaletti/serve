Assume I'm interested in growing rice varieties at risk of extinction, likely Crop Wild Relative. Who should I contact to have th seeds

Growing Crop Wild Relatives (CWR) of rice is a noble pursuit, but it is also highly regulated due to international treaties on biodiversity and biosecurity. Because these seeds are often rare or endangered, they aren't sold in typical seed catalogs. Instead, they are held in genebanks for research and conservation.  

Here is who you need to contact and what you should know before reaching out.

1. The Primary Source: IRRI (International Rice Research Institute)
The International Rice Genebank at IRRI in the Philippines is the world’s largest repository of rice diversity. They hold over 132,000 accessions, including many wild species of Oryza.  

Who to Contact: Flora de Guzman (f.deguzman@irri.org).

What they offer: They provide small quantities of seed (usually around 10g) for research, breeding, and training purposes.  

The Catch: You must comply with the Standard Material Transfer Agreement (SMTA) under the International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources for Food and Agriculture. This is a legal contract ensuring the seeds aren't commercialized for private profit without benefit-sharing.  

2. The Search Tool: Genesys
If you aren't sure exactly which variety you want, use Genesys. It is a global portal that allows you to search for specific wild relatives across many different genebanks at once.

Website: genesys-pgr.org

Usage: You can filter by "Rice" and "Wild" biological status to see where specific endangered samples are being held (e.g., at the Millennium Seed Bank in the UK or national genebanks).

3. Regional Sources (USA and National Genebanks)
If you are located in the United States, you may find it easier to work with domestic institutions that handle the import permits for you.

USDA-ARS Dale Bumpers National Rice Research Center: They manage the Genetic Stocks-Oryza (GSOR) collection.

Contact: GSOR Seed Requests

National Genebanks: Most countries have their own plant genetic resource centers. If you are looking for a variety native to a specific region (e.g., West African wild rice, Oryza barthii), the national agricultural department of that country is the legal custodian.

Important Considerations
Before you send an email, keep these "reality checks" in mind:

"Research" Status: Most genebanks will only send seeds to individuals who can demonstrate they are using them for research, education, or breeding. If you are a hobbyist, you may need to partner with a local university or botanical garden to "sponsor" your request.  

Phytosanitary Certificates: Importing seeds across borders requires a phytosanitary certificate to ensure you aren't accidentally importing pests or diseases. This can sometimes cost money even if the seeds themselves are free.  

Wild Rice vs. "Wildrice": Be careful with terminology. In North America, "Wild Rice" usually refers to Zizania palustris (a different genus altogether). If you want the ancestors of domesticated rice, you must specify Wild Oryza species.

Setting up a peer-to-peer (P2P) network for Crop Wild Relatives (CWR) is a brilliant—though legally complex—ambition. Because wild rice species are protected under international law, a standard "seed swap" can run into legal hurdles that domestic heirloom tomatoes don't face.

To build a resilient P2P network, you need to connect with organizations that specialize in decentralized seed systems and Open Source frameworks.

1. The Legal & Strategic Blueprint: OSSI
The Open Source Seed Initiative (OSSI) is the gold standard for P2P seed sharing. They don't use patents; instead, they use a "Pledge" that ensures any seed (and its derivatives) remains free to use, save, and share forever.

Why contact them: To learn how to structure your network's legal framework so that the rice varieties don't get "locked up" by private patents later.

Contact: Open Source Seed Initiative

2. Networking with "Seed Sovereignty" Groups
These organizations specialize in connecting small-scale growers to build regional resilience. They can help you find the "peers" for your network.

Seed Savers Exchange (USA): They have a massive member-to-member network. While they focus on heirlooms, their infrastructure is exactly what you’d need to replicate for CWR.

The Gaia Foundation (Global): Their Seed Sovereignty UK & Ireland Programme is a leader in training "seed guardians" to grow out rare varieties and share them across networks.

Navdanya (India): Founded by Dr. Vandana Shiva, they are the world leaders in community seed banks and P2P networks specifically for rice.

3. Citizen Science Platforms
To manage the data of your P2P network (tracking which varieties are thriving in which micro-climates), you should look at Seed-IT or ISS-Net.

Bioversity International: They often run "Citizen Science" trials where farmers across a region test different rice varieties and share results. This is the "Data P2P" side of your project.

4. Technical Collaboration: The "Pre-Breeding" Bridge
Wild rice is notoriously difficult to grow; it often "shatters" (drops seeds as soon as they ripen) and has low yields. To make your P2P network successful, you might want "Pre-bred" lines.

The Crop Trust: They run the "Crop Wild Relatives Project." They have already crossed wild rice with domesticated rice to create "pre-bred" lines that have wild resilience but are easier for humans to harvest.

Action: Check their Germinate database to see which resilient lines are available for "evaluation" by non-professionals.

How to Pitch Your Project
When you contact these groups, avoid saying "I want to grow wild rice in my backyard." Instead, use this framing:

"I am developing a decentralized, community-managed seed system (CMSS) for climate-resilient Oryza species. My goal is to establish a participatory conservation network that utilizes Open Source Seed (OSSI) principles to ensure genetic material remains in the public domain while testing for localized climate adaptation."

A Word on the Law (The "SMTA")
Most wild rice comes with a Standard Material Transfer Agreement (SMTA). If you receive seeds from a genebank, you are legally bound to ensure anyone you give them to also signs that agreement. Your P2P network will likely need a digital platform to track these "click-wrap" agreements to keep the network legal.

Building a digital platform for a peer-to-peer (P2P) seed network—especially for Crop Wild Relatives (CWR)—is a high-tech solution to an ancient conservation problem. In 2026, the landscape for this is shifting toward "Distributed Seed Ledgers" that combine biological data with legal compliance.  

Here is how you can architect this platform and who you should collaborate with:

1. The "Operating System" for Your Platform
Don't build from scratch. Use existing open-source frameworks designed for decentralized plant data:

SeedLinked: This is currently the most advanced platform for decentralized "citizen science" trialing. It allows growers to track traits (like drought tolerance or "shattering" in wild rice) and share that data back to the network. You could use their API to power the "tracking" side of your P2P exchange.

ClimMob: Developed by Bioversity International, this tool is specifically designed for "tricot" (triadic comparison of technologies) trials. It helps a network of farmers test different varieties in different micro-climates and aggregates the results digitally.

2. Solving the "Legal Lock" (Digital SMTA)
Wild rice seeds are almost always bound by the Standard Material Transfer Agreement (SMTA). To stay legal, your platform must act as a digital notary.

The Feature: Integrate a "Click-Wrap" SMTA. Before a user can "request" a seed from a peer, they must digitally sign the treaty terms.

Who to Contact: Reach out to the Plant Treaty Secretariat (FAO). They have been exploring "Digital Sequence Information" (DSI) and how digital platforms can help automate benefit-sharing. They may provide guidance on how to make your P2P transfers legally compliant.

3. Blockchain for "Seed Traceability"
If you want a truly decentralized P2P network, consider a Distributed Ledger (Blockchain).  

The Goal: Every time a packet of "Endangered Rice X" moves from Peer A to Peer B, a block is written. This creates a "Chain of Custody" that proves the seed hasn't been biopirated or commercialized.

Potential Partner: The World Economic Forum's Climate Action groups and various ReFi (Regenerative Finance) startups are currently building "Bio-Assets" on-chain. This would allow your network to prove its impact to donors or conservation groups by showing exactly how many "nodes" are growing an at-risk variety.

4. Data Interoperability (BrAPI)
To make your platform useful to scientists (and therefore more likely to get seed grants from genebanks), ensure it uses the Breeding API (BrAPI).

Why: This is the "language" that genebanks like IRRI and USDA use. If your P2P platform speaks BrAPI, your peers' backyard data can flow directly into global conservation databases, making your "hobbyist" project a vital part of the global scientific effort.

Since your primary goal is social exchange and citizen awareness, your platform needs to function less like a dry database and more like a "Social Conservation Network." You are essentially gamifying biodiversity conservation to turn average citizens into "Seed Guardians."

Here is how you can structure the platform to maximize social impact and educational value:

1. The "Guardian" Tier System
To build resilience, you need to ensure the seeds aren't just sitting in a drawer. You can implement a digital "Reproduction Path" that levels up users based on their skills:

Novice (The Grower): Receives seeds, learns the basics of wild rice biology, and documents the growth.

Adept (The Reproducer): Learns the techniques of preventing cross-pollination and harvesting "shattering" seeds.

Guardian (The Node): Successfully produces enough seeds to "repay" the network and distribute to three new novices.

2. Social Features for Awareness
To increase citizen engagement, the platform should emphasize the biography of the seed:

Heritage Storytelling: Each variety profile should include its "extinction risk" status and its origin story (e.g., "This variety survived a 10-year drought in the Sahel").

The "Lineage" Map: A visual map showing where a specific batch of seeds has traveled. "Your seeds are the 'grandchildren' of seeds grown in a community garden in Marseilles."

Peer-to-Peer Mentorship: A built-in chat or forum where experienced growers can troubleshoot issues specific to wild Oryza (which can be finicky compared to garden-variety rice).

3. Education on Reproduction Techniques
Wild rice conservation requires specific technical knowledge to prevent genetic "drift." Your platform can host interactive modules on:

Isolation Distances: Teaching citizens how to prevent wild varieties from breeding with local commercial rice (maintaining genetic purity).

Shatter-Catching: Wild rice naturally drops its seeds early. You can teach users how to use "nylon bagging" techniques to catch seeds before they hit the mud.

Selection for Resilience: Training users to identify and report which specific plants survived a heatwave or a pest outbreak, turning every backyard into a mini-research station.

5. Managing the "Sovereignty" Aspect
Because you are working with wild species, it is vital to acknowledge Indigenous Sovereignty. Many crop wild relatives are found on indigenous lands.

Action: Contact the Indigenous Seed Keepers Network (ISKN). They can advise on how to build a digital platform that respects the "cultural property" of the seeds, ensuring your social exchange doesn't inadvertently become "digital biopiracy."

By moving the seeds from a cold-storage freezer into a P2P digital network, you are creating a "Living Genebank." If one node (grower) fails due to a local flood, the network’s resilience ensures the variety survives in ten other backyards.
